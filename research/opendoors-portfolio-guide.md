# Open Doors portfolio research and polishing guide

Research date: 23 September 2026. Purpose: a working reference for a later review of this portfolio.

This synthesis draws on 24 public Open Doors articles: 15 individual portfolio showcases, eight advice articles, and the 2025 retrospective. The sample includes the latest showcase, Itzie Pulido, published on the research date. It is a substantial selected sample, not an exhaustive archive. Research used the public article text; it does not constitute hands-on testing of the featured portfolio sites or access to private case studies, paid reviews, or video recordings.

The observations belong to Florian Bölter's reviews. The audit rubric and suggested workflow below are my synthesis, not an official Open Doors scoring system. This file does not yet evaluate the user's portfolio.

## The central principle

Make it easy for a reviewer to identify your strengths, find relevant work, understand your decisions, and believe your contribution. Visual execution, information hierarchy, project selection, and evidence all participate in that judgment. A striking homepage cannot compensate for inaccessible or poorly explained work. [How hiring managers scan](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-hiring-managers-actually-scan-your-portfolio-in-2026)

## Recurring advice we can apply

### 1. Position around demonstrated strengths

State what kind of designer you are and the kinds of problems you handle. Generic declarations about passion or meaningful experiences provide little useful distinction. Every prominent claim should have visible support in the projects. [First-scan rejection signals](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/5-reasons-i-close-a-junior-portfolio-in-30-seconds)

Positioning does not require a new website for every application. Build a coherent direction across the portfolio and resume, then make small adjustments where relevant. Problem types such as complex workflows or decision-heavy interfaces can communicate a specialty without restricting you to one industry. [Positioning guide](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/the-guide-to-position-yourself-for-the-right-roles-in-2026)

### 2. Curate for the work you want next

Select projects that support your intended roles and your current level. Earlier work should earn its place again. Three or four substantial projects are a recurring recommendation, not a quota. An adjacent discipline helps when the relationship to your main practice is understandable. [Choosing case studies](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/choosing-the-right-case-studies-for-your-portfolio)

Give flagship work, supporting experiments, and personal material different levels of prominence. The reviewer should not have to decide which of seven equally weighted projects best represents you. [Madhurima review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-madhurima-chatterjee)

### 3. Put work within immediate reach

A short introduction followed by work is a strong default. Avoid making people cross another page, long biography, or elaborate interaction before reaching the projects. The author explicitly says tool choice is secondary to execution; using Framer is not a hiring requirement. [Ultimate portfolio guide](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/the-ultimate-portfolio-guide-for-product-designers-in-2026)

Native case-study pages preserve the reading experience. External Figma prototypes and slide decks add loading, navigation, and context-switching costs. An interview deck also assumes a presenter who can explain it; a public portfolio must communicate independently. [Inika review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-inika-jhamvar), [Yash review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-yash-fataniya)

### 4. Make project cards informative and desirable

A product name alone seldom explains the problem or contribution. Show a recognizable piece of the product and enough context to justify opening the project. [How hiring managers scan](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-hiring-managers-actually-scan-your-portfolio-in-2026)

Check asset sharpness, crop, device proportions, UI scale, alignment, and responsive behavior. The first preview is already evidence of your attention to detail. Put relevant work where the intended audience can find it. [First-scan rejection signals](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/5-reasons-i-close-a-junior-portfolio-in-30-seconds)

### 5. Write a story that survives scanning

Build around the problem, your contribution, and what happened. Start by gathering material, grouping the important moments, and cutting repetition. Write meaningful headings before supporting paragraphs. Show research findings and the decisions they changed; a large research board rarely communicates that on its own. Add body text where headings and visuals need help. [Case-study writing guide](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-to-write-a-strong-case-study-for-your-portfolio-in-2026)

Generic process labels can still work as small navigation labels when stronger narrative headings carry the actual meaning. A visitor should understand the arc without reading every sentence. [Caleb review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-caleb-wu)

### 6. Show judgment and credible impact

Explain constraints, alternatives, changes of direction, and tradeoffs. The interesting evidence is why a decision happened and what it changed. [How hiring managers scan](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-hiring-managers-actually-scan-your-portfolio-in-2026)

Distinguish activity counts from outcomes: making prototypes and speaking to stakeholders describe work performed; improving a workflow or getting a proposal adopted describes a consequence. [Lana review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-lana-farkas)

Where exact metrics are unavailable, seek substantiated directional or qualitative evidence. Put useful outcomes near the beginning. Ownership and adaptability become more convincing when their effects are visible. [Giang review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-giang-tran)

### 7. Use motion to explain behavior

Short recordings can replace confusing grids of interface states. The video guide suggests roughly 20–30 seconds for a flow and generally favors quiet playback. It warns against many competing autoplay previews; selective playback or a layout with one focal project can help. These are editorial preferences, not universal timing requirements. [Video guide](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-to-show-off-your-work-better-with-video)

A moving slideshow is not necessarily an interaction demonstration. A useful clip makes the action, response, and progression intelligible. Keep details large enough to inspect and transitions slow enough to follow. [Itzie review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-itzie-pulido)

### 8. Make complex visuals readable without extra work

Crop to the relevant control, divide long workflows into stages, and simplify dense diagrams. A lightbox does not repair a first view that communicates too little. Also consider which project attracts attention: one animated card can overpower more relevant static work. [Jessica review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-jessica-hsu)

### 9. Demonstrate realistic product states

Consider empty, loading, no-results, validation, failure, permission, partial-success, and recovery situations where relevant. Show a few consequential examples rather than an exhaustive inventory. Good recovery explains what happened, its consequence, and the next action. A prototype can demonstrate this thinking without a production backend. [Show me what happens when it breaks](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/show-me-what-happens-when-it-breaks)

### 10. Give personality and experiments a purpose

An About page can make the designer memorable through specific interests, photographs, or a considered point of view. Personal material works best when the main work has already established credibility. [Sophie review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-sophie-manalo)

Experiments need lightweight context: what they are, why they exist, what you owned, and whether they are built, shipped, or exploratory. A small caption can reveal capabilities that visuals alone leave ambiguous. [Sophia review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-sophia-liu), [Justin Shi review](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-justin-shi)

## Nuances worth preserving

- **There is no required project count.** Caleb demonstrates the potential of two exceptional projects; Queenie demonstrates coherent breadth. Choose based on evidence and quality, not a numerical target. [Caleb](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-caleb-wu), [Queenie](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-queenie-hsiao)
- **Short does not mean shallow.** Madhurima's more conventional, substantial case studies are praised because their structure and synthesis justify the material. [Madhurima](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-madhurima-chatterjee)
- **A playful exception is not a default pattern.** Lana's themed unfinished-project interaction earns praise, while Sophie's generic unfinished card is criticized. Our default should still be to feature work that rewards opening it. [Lana](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-lana-farkas), [Sophie](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-sophie-manalo)
- **Strong craft does not guarantee employment.** The 2025 retrospective includes highly praised designers still seeking roles at publication. Its examples support presentation lessons, not causal promises about hiring. [2025 retrospective](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/the-best-junior-design-portfolios-i-saw-in-2025)
- **Tool promotion needs context.** Showcase pages disclose affiliate links, including Framer promotion. The general guide explicitly prioritizes execution over platform. Nothing here establishes a reason to migrate this portfolio's stack. [Ultimate guide](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/the-ultimate-portfolio-guide-for-product-designers-in-2026), [Itzie disclosure](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-itzie-pulido)

## Working rubric for our later audit

This rubric translates the research into observable checks. It is not a hiring predictor. Mark each item **clear**, **partial**, **missing**, or **not applicable**, and record the page or artifact supporting the judgment.

| Area | Evidence we will inspect | Priority if weak |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Access | Main work opens; no broken destinations or misleading interactive elements. | P0 |
| Positioning | Introduction names a useful strength that projects substantiate. | P1 |
| Selection | Strong, relevant projects lead; secondary work has an intelligible purpose. | P1 |
| Preview | Product, contribution, and reason to open are apparent; assets are legible. | P1 |
| Story | Headings and visuals explain the problem, intervention, and consequence. | P1 |
| Ownership | Reader can separate individual decisions from team accomplishments. | P1 |
| Judgment | Constraints, alternatives, and at least one consequential decision are visible. | P1 |
| Evidence | Outcomes have support; activity, measured results, and expectations are distinguishable. | P1 |
| Visual craft | Repeated elements follow consistent type, spacing, alignment, and image rules. | P2 |
| Behavior | Motion explains the product; controls and transitions feel deliberate. | P2 |
| Range | Experiments have enough context to demonstrate additional capability. | P2 |
| Personality | Specific human details enrich a clear professional profile. | P3 |

P0 means a blocker to accessing or understanding the work. P1 means core hiring evidence. P2 means presentation and supporting proof. P3 means optional enrichment. A severe readability problem should be raised above P2.

## Suggested polishing sequence

The following is our proposed workflow, rather than a prescribed sequence from a single article.

1. **Establish direction.** Identify intended roles and the strongest supported capabilities before rewriting the introduction.
2. **Inventory evidence.** For each candidate project, list your role, team, constraints, status, key decisions, visuals, and available outcomes. Flag missing information explicitly.
3. **Select and order.** Assign projects to flagship work, supporting work, or material to keep outside the main presentation.
4. **Test the first impression.** Review the introduction and initial project previews together. Confirm they tell the same professional story.
5. **Restructure one flagship study.** Build its narrative headings and visual sequence, then add only the explanatory text it needs. Establish a reusable presentation pattern from real content.
6. **Verify claims.** Distinguish shipped results, observations from testing, qualitative feedback, and expected benefits. For numbers, retain the source, measurement context, and limits of attribution. Never turn a target into an achieved result.
7. **Polish assets and behavior.** Improve crops, scale, typography, spacing, recordings, and consistent interaction details after the story works.
8. **Check the complete experience.** Inspect desktop and mobile, links, navigation, media loading, keyboard access, focus visibility, contrast, and motion preferences. These accessibility and implementation checks are additional practical QA recommendations, not claims that every cited review covers them.
9. **Run a short comprehension test.** Let another person scan a study for approximately 30 seconds, then ask what problem it addressed, what you contributed, and what changed. The author recommends a brief recall test; our three questions make it actionable. [Ultimate guide](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/the-ultimate-portfolio-guide-for-product-designers-in-2026)

## Case-study drafting aid

This is a flexible editorial aid. We should adapt it to each project's actual evidence.

| Part | What it should establish |
| --- | --- |
| Opening | The product, user problem, your role, and a representative result or interaction. |
| Stakes | Why the problem mattered and which constraint made it difficult. |
| Turning points | Two or three decisions that changed the work, each paired with evidence. |
| Solution | Readable UI details or a short flow demonstrating those decisions. |
| Consequence | What shipped, improved, was validated, or remains uncertain. |
| Reflection | A specific lesson or next validation step grounded in the project. |

For each turning point, draft: **observation → decision → reason → evidence → consequence**. This is our synthesis of the recurring emphasis on decisions and clear visual support.

Illustrative heading transformations below are invented examples, not claims about the user's work:

| Generic label | More informative heading |
| --- | --- |
| Research | Operators could find alerts, but could not tell which needed action. |
| Design process | We tested inline editing to keep users in the workflow. |
| Final design | The new review screen brings exceptions into one view. |
| Results | The prototype is ready for task-based validation. |

The last example deliberately avoids implying a measured benefit before validation. We should choose wording from actual evidence rather than filling a template with persuasive but unsupported claims.
